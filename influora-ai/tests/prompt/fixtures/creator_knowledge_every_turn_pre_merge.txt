Category playbooks (find the creator's category here first; obey its Never say line):
- Beauty & skincare: formats: GRWM (Get Ready With Me), Step-by-step tutorial, Myth-busting, Honest product review. Hook angle: Open on the visible result or a common misconception, then walk back to show how you got there or why it's wrong. Structure: Before-After-Bridge (BAB). Camera: Close-up, Extreme close-up, Static / locked-off shot. Never say: No medical, treatment or 'cure' claims (acne, pigmentation, hair fall), even framed as 'it worked for me', unless you hold and show a relevant qualification. No 'clinically proven' language and no promised result timelines.
- Fashion: formats: GRWM outfit video, Try-on haul, Styling series, Outfit transition reel. Hook angle: Open on the finished look or a 'how do I style this' question, then show the transformation or breakdown. Structure: Before-After-Bridge (BAB). Camera: Wide / establishing shot, Medium shot, Whip pan. Never say: No unverified claims about fabric quality or 'cheapest / best price' comparisons, and no promotion of counterfeit or replica products.
- Food: formats: Recipe tutorial, Street food vlog, Regional cuisine feature, Quick recipe short. Hook angle: Open on the finished dish or the most visually striking step, then walk through how you got there. Structure: Three-act structure. Camera: Close-up, Cutaway / insert shot, Static / locked-off shot. Never say: No medical or 'cures' / 'boosts immunity' claims, no 'clinically proven' language, and no specific nutrition numbers unless you can cite a real source. Say 'this is what worked for me' when sharing a personal experience.
- Fitness: formats: Home workout tutorial, What I eat in a day, Form-check demo, Motivational journey. Hook angle: Open with the movement or result the viewer wants, then show the how - including proper form. Structure: Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS). Camera: Wide / establishing shot, Medium shot, Close-up. Never say: No weight-loss-in-X-days claims, before/after promises or guaranteed results, and no diet, supplement or injury advice unless you hold and show a relevant qualification. No medical, 'cure' or 'clinically proven' claims. Say 'this is my routine', not 'this will work for you'.
- Tech & gadgets: formats: Unboxing, Honest review, Comparison video, Feature tutorial. Hook angle: Open on the product reveal or the single most surprising feature, then move into the full evaluation. Structure: StoryBrand (7-part). Camera: Close-up, Extreme close-up, Cutaway / insert shot. Never say: No unverified performance claims ('fastest', 'best') and no misleading comparisons that favor a sponsor. Disclose the partnership clearly regardless of how the review reads.
- Personal finance: formats: Budget tips, What I spend breakdown, Investing-basics explainer, Skit-style money lesson. Hook angle: Open with a relatable money habit or question, then walk through the practical fix. Structure: Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS). Camera: Medium shot, Two-shot, Static / locked-off shot. Never say: No stock or fund tips, no 'guaranteed returns', and nothing framed as personal financial advice. ASCI asks for a SEBI registration number for stock or investment content, and SEBI has rules on which creators regulated brands (brokers, fund houses) may work with; check the current SEBI and ASCI guidelines before any finance deal. Keep market data general, not tied to recent prices.
- Travel: formats: Destination vlog, Budget travel guide, Solo travel safety guide, Food + travel crossover. Hook angle: Open on the most visually striking destination moment, then walk through the practical how-to-get-there details. Structure: Narrative transportation. Camera: Wide / establishing shot, Tracking / follow shot, Point-of-view (POV) shot. Never say: No absolute safety claims ('completely safe') and no cost claims without noting prices can change over time. Disclose sponsored stays or hotels clearly.
- Comedy & entertainment: formats: Character sketch, Relatable daily-life scenario, Pop-culture parody, Storytelling comedy. Hook angle: Open straight into the exaggerated or relatable moment - no setup - then let the punchline land. Structure: Character archetypes (hero/mentor/guide). Camera: Medium shot, Reaction shot, Whip pan. Never say: No mocking real, identifiable individuals without consent, no content that could read as misinformation dressed as satire, and disclose clearly when a sketch is a paid brand integration.
- Parenting: formats: Parenting tips, Day-in-the-life, Parenting hack, Q&A session. Hook angle: Open with a relatable parenting moment or question, then share what worked for your family. Structure: StoryBrand (7-part). Camera: Medium shot, Close-up, Two-shot. Never say: No medical advice for children, and no showing children's private details (school name, face-identifiable location, full name in text). Say 'this is what worked for us' rather than framing any one method as the only right way.
- Lifestyle: formats: Routine vlog, Morning/night routine, Room tour, Organization tips. Hook angle: Open with the transformed space or the most satisfying moment of the routine, then walk through how you got there. Structure: Before-After-Bridge (BAB). Camera: Wide / establishing shot, Medium shot, Close-up, Tracking / follow shot. Never say: No claims about productivity or lifestyle changes guaranteeing specific outcomes, and no promotion of expensive products as 'must-haves'. Say 'this is what worked for me' when sharing personal routines.
- Wellness: formats: Meditation guide, Mental health story, Self-care routine, Stress management tips. Hook angle: Open with a relatable wellness struggle or question, then share what helped you personally. Structure: Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS). Camera: Medium shot, Close-up, Static / locked-off shot. Never say: No medical claims, no 'cure' or 'treat' language for any health condition, no 'clinically proven' statements, and no diagnosis or treatment advice. Mental health content should encourage professional help when needed. Say 'this helped me' rather than prescribing solutions.
- Gaming: formats: Gameplay walkthrough, Game review, Tips and tricks, Live stream highlight. Hook angle: Open with the most exciting gameplay moment or the problem you're solving, then show the strategy. Structure: Three-act structure. Camera: Close-up, Point-of-view (POV) shot, Reaction shot, Cutaway / insert shot. Never say: No promotion of real-money gaming, betting, or gambling platforms. ASCI has specific guidelines on gaming and betting advertising - check the current version before posting any gaming content that involves stakes or rewards. No promises about winning or earning through games.
- Education: formats: Study tips, Exam preparation guide, Concept explainer, Book/course review. Hook angle: Open with a common study struggle or question, then walk through the practical technique or resource. Structure: Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS). Camera: Medium shot, Close-up, Cutaway / insert shot, Static / locked-off shot. Never say: No guaranteed marks, scores, ranks, or job placement promises. No 'crack any exam' or 'guaranteed selection' language. Focus on study techniques and resources that helped you personally, not promised outcomes.

Brand deals on Influora (paid posts for a brand):
- Ad disclosure label: ASCI asks for a clear, upfront disclosure label - such as 'Ad' or 'Paid partnership' - visible without needing to click 'more'. Use the platform's own paid-partnership tool as well as an on-screen label. Guidelines are updated periodically, so check ASCI's current version before posting. For video: Put the label on screen as on-screen text from the start of the video, not only in the caption, and keep it up for as long as ASCI's current guidelines ask for a video of your length. Write it in the same language as the video. Also turn on the platform's paid-partnership tag.
- Only endorse what you've used: Good practice (reflected in CCPA endorsement guidance) is to only endorse a product or service you have actually used or experienced yourself, not just repeated brand claims. If a brand hasn't sent you the product, ask for it before agreeing to the deal. For video: Base the script on your own hands-on experience with the product - an actual first impression or usage moment on camera - rather than reading out brand-supplied claims you haven't verified yourself.
- Influora draft-to-payment flow: On Influora, the brand has 3 working days to review your draft - after that, the Influora team steps in. You get up to 2 rounds of revisions. Once it's approved, post it live and share the link on Influora; you're paid by bank transfer within 2 working days of that live link. For video: Plan your shoot-to-post schedule around this flow: submit drafts with enough runway for the 3-working-day review, use your revision rounds to align with feedback, then post live and share the link on Influora promptly since that link starts the payment countdown.
- Health and finance content need disclosed expertise: For health, nutrition, banking, finance or insurance content, ASCI asks that technical advice or claims (a treatment, cure, prevention, returns, a specific investment) come only from creators who hold the relevant qualification and show it upfront; for stocks or investments that includes a SEBI registration number. General, non-technical mentions usually don't need this. Check ASCI's current guidelines for your case. For video: Without the relevant qualification, keep a health or finance brand video general (for example, why regular check-ups matter) and make no treatment, cure or returns claim, not even as 'it worked for me'. If you are qualified, show it on screen at the start.
- Disclosure required even for genuine reviews: ASCI asks for a disclosure label whenever there's a material connection with a brand (a free product, payment or other benefit), even when your review is completely genuine. Saying 'I wasn't paid for this' doesn't replace the label if you did get something. Check ASCI's current guidelines. For video: Add the disclosure label any time a brand gave you the product free or paid you for the video, no matter how independent or positive your actual opinion is - disclosure is about the connection, not the verdict.

Storytelling structures:
- StoryBrand (7-part): Character (the audience is the hero) -> Problem (external, internal, philosophical) -> Guide (the brand, positioned as mentor not hero) -> Plan (a clear, simple path forward) -> Call to action -> Success (what happens if they follow the plan) -> Failure (what happens if they don't). For video: Frame the creator/brand as the guide and the viewer as the hero with a problem; the video becomes the 'plan', the CTA is explicit, and showing both the payoff and the cost of inaction increases follow-through.
- Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS): State the problem the audience has -> Agitate it — make the pain concrete and immediate -> Solve it with your content/product. For video: Classic short-form hook structure: name the pain point in the first line, twist the knife briefly, then deliver the fix — works especially well for 'mistake' and 'myth-busting' video formats.
- Before-After-Bridge (BAB): Show the 'before' state (the struggle) -> Show the 'after' state (the transformation/result) -> Show the 'bridge' — how to get from one to the other. For video: Ideal for transformation content (fitness, skincare, home organization) — open on the after/result to hook curiosity, then walk back to show the bridge.
- Three-act structure: Setup (introduce character/situation) -> Confrontation (the obstacle or complication) -> Resolution (the outcome). For video: Even a 60-second video benefits from a compressed 3-act shape: a few seconds of setup, the bulk of the video on the obstacle/attempt, and a clear payoff at the end.
- Newsjacking: Monitor breaking news/trends in your niche -> Move fast — the window is hours, not days -> Attach your brand/expertise to the trend with a genuine angle, not a forced tie-in. For video: React to a trending topic in your niche (a viral claim, a new product, a seasonal event) within the trend's live window to capture algorithmic reach that evergreen content can't get on its own.
- Buyer persona targeting: Define the specific audience segment (not 'everyone') -> Identify their language, pain points, and platform habits -> Tailor the story's problem/solution to that exact segment. For video: The more specific the audience, the easier it is to speak to them. 'Home workouts for small city flats' speaks to a clearer group than 'fitness', because those viewers share the same limits (little space, busy days). Decide who you're talking to, then shape the problem and the fix for that group.
- Direct-to-audience publishing: Publish directly to your audience instead of relying on gatekeepers (press, algorithms alone) -> Build owned channels (email list, community) alongside platform reach -> Use consistency and authenticity over polish. For video: Encourages creators to treat YouTube/Instagram as a direct relationship channel — consistent posting and community reply builds compounding trust beyond any single video's performance.
- Freytag's Pyramid (5-part dramatic arc): Exposition (setup) -> Rising action (building complication) -> Climax (peak tension/turning point) -> Falling action (consequences unfold) -> Resolution (final outcome). For video: A more granular alternative to the 3-act structure for longer-form videos (5-10+ min) — gives two extra beats (rising/falling action) to build and release tension around a demonstration or story.
- Character archetypes (hero/mentor/guide): Hero — the one with the goal (your viewer) -> Mentor/Guide — the one who helps (your brand/creator) -> Threshold/obstacle — what stands in the way. For video: Mirrors StoryBrand's Character+Guide structure; useful when scripting a video as a mini-journey rather than a flat list of tips — gives the viewer a role to see themselves in.
- Narrative transportation: Draw the viewer into a story world (vivid, specific detail) -> Reduce their critical/skeptical distance while 'inside' the story -> Deliver the message/CTA while transportation is highest. For video: Explains why a specific, concrete anecdote from the creator's own life (one they have told you) out-persuades a generic claim — specificity increases immersion, which increases persuasion.
- Grab-Story-CTA (micro 3-part structure): Grab: a short, specific first line that earns attention -> Story: a brief, emotional moment or message -> Call to action: one clear, named next step. For video: A tighter alternative to PAS for very short videos and captions: go from a striking first line straight into the emotional moment, then one named action ('share this with a friend who is starting out', not just 'share').

Camera angles and shots:
- Wide / establishing shot (orientation): Orients the viewer in the space before anything happens; shows full body plus surroundings so a viewer can copy a movement or judge scale. Use when: Opening shot of a tutorial or demo; any moment you need the audience to see the whole setup (e.g. a home-workout routine in a small room).
- Medium shot (orientation): Balances context and facial expression; the default 'talking and doing' shot. Use when: Main narration/demo shots where you need to see both the presenter's face and their hands/body doing something.
- Close-up (detail): Heightens emotional intensity or draws attention to a specific detail (face, product, hands). Use when: Emotional beats, product reveals, or 'watch exactly how I do this' form-check moments.
- Extreme close-up (detail): Isolates one detail to create tension, curiosity, or precision (an eye, an ingredient, a number on a screen). Use when: Hook moments in the first 3 seconds, or a single critical detail (a mistake, a result number).
- Point-of-view (POV) shot (perspective): Puts the viewer directly in the subject's perspective, increasing immersion and empathy. Use when: Showing 'what you'll see when you do this' in a tutorial, or building suspense in a story-driven video.
- Over-the-shoulder shot (perspective): Frames a conversation or demonstration from behind one participant, keeping both people connected in-frame. Use when: Interviews, two-person dialogue, or a 'watching someone do something' teaching moment.
- Low angle (power_dynamics): Camera looks up at the subject, making them appear powerful, confident, or dominant. Use when: Authority-building moments (a coach demonstrating expertise, a confident brand statement).
- High angle (power_dynamics): Camera looks down at the subject, making them appear smaller, vulnerable, or relatable. Use when: Showing struggle, vulnerability, or a 'before' state in a transformation story.
- Dutch / tilted angle (mood): Tilts the horizon to create unease, disorientation, or energy. Use when: Comedic beats, chaotic moments, or 'something's off' storytelling cues. Use sparingly — overuse reads as amateurish.
- Dolly-in / push-in (movement): Camera moves slowly toward the subject, building emotional intensity and drawing the viewer into a moment. Use when: Reveal moments, emotional peaks, or the climax of a mistake/lesson story.
- Dolly-out / pull-out (movement): Camera moves away from the subject, revealing context or creating a sense of resolution. Use when: Ending shots, revealing the full result of a transformation, or comedic context-reveals.
- Tracking / follow shot (movement): Camera moves alongside the subject, maintaining energy and continuity of action. Use when: Walk-and-talk intros, action B-roll, or following a process from start to finish.
- Handheld (texture): Slight camera instability reads as authentic, urgent, or 'in the moment' — the opposite of polished. Use when: Vlogging, behind-the-scenes segments, or moments meant to feel raw/unscripted.
- Static / locked-off shot (texture): A perfectly still frame lets the action or performance carry the shot without camera distraction. Use when: Tutorial demonstration shots where the viewer needs to study the movement without camera noise.
- Whip pan (transition): A fast, blurred camera swing that creates an energetic or comedic transition between two shots. Use when: Fast-paced cuts between scenes, comedic beats, or high-energy Shorts/Reels editing.
- Cutaway / insert shot (supporting): A brief shot of a supporting detail (hands, an object, a phone screen) that adds information without breaking the main shot's continuity. Use when: Showing an ingredient, a product feature, or a step's result while the main narration continues.
- Reaction shot (emotion): Shows a person's emotional response to something just seen or said, building empathy and pacing. Use when: Testimonial moments, comedic timing, or letting a 'wow' reaction sell a result.
- Two-shot (relationship): Frames two people together in one shot, visually establishing their relationship or dynamic. Use when: Collaboration content, interviews, or family/relationship-driven storytelling.
- Color spot (framing): Dressing a subject or key prop in one standout color against a plainer background makes them instantly recognizable, even blurred or distant. Use when: Helping a viewer track a specific person, product or team color across a busy or fast-cut sequence.
- Sharp subject, soft crowd (framing): Keeping only the main subject in sharp focus and better lit than everyone else around them makes them the obvious point of interest in a busy scene. Use when: Market, event, or street content where you want your subject (or a specific person/product) to stand out from a crowded background.
- Step into the light (lighting): Letting the subject walk from shadow into light, with the camera tilting up as their face becomes visible, gives a simple arrival real weight. Use when: An entrance moment for a person, a transformation reveal, or the start of a dramatic story segment.
- Turn-and-approach (movement): Starting with the subject walking away, then turning to walk straight at the camera, signals a change of mind or a new decision without needing narration. Use when: A 'then I decided...' beat in a storytime video, or a transition into a confident brand statement.
- Low-to-wide reveal (reveal): Starting tight and low, then panning and moving behind the subject as they crouch or stop, reveals a much bigger space or challenge than the audience expected. Use when: Showing the true scale of a task, a crowd, or a venue right after a close, secretive-feeling opening.
- Mirror reveal (reveal): A mirror in frame lets a second subject or detail appear and be noticed in the same shot, without cutting away and back. Use when: Getting-ready or room-tour content where something (a person, a mess, a surprise) is revealed through a mirror behind you.
- Open-and-reveal (reveal): Builds anticipation by hiding the subject behind a closed door, box, curtain or bag, then opening it to reveal them or the product centered in frame. Use when: Product unboxings, outfit reveals, or 'guess what's inside' openers where the opening itself is the hook.
- Parting reveal (reveal): Two people or objects that start blocking the view move apart like curtains, opening up the frame to show what was hidden behind them. Use when: A crowd or group parting to reveal a product, guest, or result -- works well as a mid-video payoff beat.
- Walk-in behind you (reveal): Lets something important appear behind the main subject while the front of the frame barely moves, so the audience discovers it themselves instead of being told about it. Use when: A 'wait for it' reel where a person, pet, or product quietly walks into frame behind you while you keep talking to camera.
- Follow-the-object pan (transition): Following a handed-off or carried object as it moves gives the camera a natural reason to pan from one person or area to another, instead of an random camera movement with no clear reason. Use when: Moving between two people or setups in one continuous shot -- for example following a product as it's passed to a friend for a reaction.

Narrative principles:
- Core story / raison d'être: Every brand or creator has an underlying 'why' — the founding reason and values behind what they do — and this core story should ground every piece of content, even when only a simplified version is shown externally. For video: Before scripting individual videos, define your channel's one-sentence 'why' (e.g. 'I make fitness accessible for people with no time or equipment'). Every video's hook and CTA should trace back to that core story so the channel feels coherent over time, not like disconnected one-offs.
- Operational vs strategic story levels: Organizations (and by extension creators) tell a simple, external-facing story day-to-day, while a more complex internal story explains the full context of decisions and direction. For video: The short hook/caption is your 'operational' story (simple, external); your channel's about section or a longer-form video can carry the fuller 'strategic' story of why you make this content — keep both consistent with each other.
- Four functions of storytelling: Storytelling in an organization serves four distinct functions: internal leadership (aligning a team around shared values), marketing communication (engaging an audience emotionally), external stakeholder narratives (how others talk about you, which you don't fully control), and cultural sensemaking (helping people understand their own identity through the story). For video: A creator's storytelling operates in at least two of these lanes: marketing communication (the video itself) and cultural sensemaking (why the audience relates to and shares it) — a video that only sells but doesn't help the audience make sense of their own situation misses the sensemaking function that drives sharing.
- Self-brand connection (sensemaking): A brand or piece of content becomes more meaningful to someone the more closely it connects to their own sense of identity — people process content partly to satisfy their own psychological need to make sense of their world. For video: Content that helps a viewer understand their own situation (e.g. 'why you're always tired even after 8 hours of sleep') creates a deeper connection than content that only demonstrates a product or skill, because it ties into the viewer's self-understanding.
- Story building blocks (audience, message, conflict, character, plot, action): Across the literature, the recurring elements needed for an effective story are: a clearly understood target audience, a clear message, a conflict/challenge, relatable characters, a plot, and a resulting action. For video: A practical pre-shoot checklist: name your audience specifically, state your one message in a sentence, name the conflict/pain point, decide who the 'character' is (usually the viewer or the creator), sketch the plot beats, and confirm the action you want the viewer to take.
- Repetition as the true success signal: Genuine storytelling success isn't just the audience showing interest once — it's when they repeat or retell the story themselves, which signals real engagement and impact. For video: Track not just views/likes but whether viewers repeat your framing in comments, remixes, or their own captions — that's a stronger signal of a hook or story actually landing than raw view count alone.
- Conflict as the engine of a story: Audiences connect more with a real problem, setback or challenge than with a polished success story alone; showing the struggle makes a creator or brand feel approachable and human, not just impressive. For video: In a Reel or Short, show the messy middle — a mistake, a doubt, a hard choice — before the payoff, instead of jumping straight to the finished result.
- Credibility through real, unstaged detail: A story earns trust when it uses specific, real details from an actual experience rather than invented or overly polished scenes; anything that feels scripted or fictional weakens believability. For video: Keep small real details in a brand-collab video — an actual first reaction, an actual location, an actual message — instead of a generic staged unboxing line.
- Consistency between what you say and what you do: A story only holds up if it matches behavior everywhere the audience sees you — captions, comments, DMs, the product itself — not only the words in one polished video. For video: Only say things like 'I use this daily' in a brand-collab video if they are true, and make sure your other posts, replies and past content don't contradict it.
- Treat the audience as a participant, not just a viewer: People today expect to react, comment and shape a creator's or brand's story, not just receive it passively; consistently responding to that reaction builds a loyal community over time. For video: Turn a recurring comment or question from your audience into the topic of your next Reel or Short, and credit the commenter, so followers see their input shaping your content.
- Listen to your audience before creating: Before creating new content, pay attention to how your audience is already talking about you — comments, shares, their own stories about you — to check whether your last story actually landed. For video: Scroll your comments and saves before scripting the next video; if people keep asking the same question or misunderstanding a point, address that in your next Reel or Short.
- Story matters more once competitors look alike: When a product or niche is new, the offering itself can carry attention; once many creators or brands offer something similar, a distinct, consistent story becomes the main way to stand out. For video: If your niche (e.g. skincare, fitness) is crowded with similar creators, lean harder into your specific story and point of view in videos rather than only demonstrating the product like everyone else.
- Origin story as an anchor for trust: Sharing how and why you started — the spark, the early struggle — gives new followers or brand partners a reason to trust you beyond the current content, because they understand the ‘why’ behind it. For video: Post an occasional Reel or YouTube video on ‘why I started this page/channel’, including a real early struggle, to give new followers and prospective brand partners context.
- Research audience pain points before scripting: Understanding the specific frustrations and needs of your followers or a brand's customers, before writing a script, makes the story land because it addresses something real rather than a generic topic. For video: Before filming, list the 2-3 problems your audience or the brand's customers keep mentioning in comments/DMs, then build the video's hook around solving one of them.
- Feature audience stories as proof: Sharing a real follower's or customer's experience, in their own words, builds more trust than a creator or brand only talking about themselves, because it shows the outcome through someone else's eyes. For video: With the person's clear permission, include a short real clip or quote from a follower who actually used the product in a brand-collab video. Hide their personal details, don't edit their words, and skip any health, result or earnings claims the brand can't back up.
- Community reactions as content fuel: Comments, remixes, reshares and reactions from your own audience are a source of story material, and folding them back into new content feels more genuine than only brand-originated messaging. For video: Turn a striking comment, remix or reaction to your last video into the opening beat of your next Reel or Short, showing you saw and responded to it.
- Write for ONE hyper-specific person: Rather than targeting a broad group, picture one very specific real person (age, life stage, daily routine, worries, what they enjoy) and talk to them directly. Content made for one clear person reads as personal instead of generic. For video: Before scripting, write a short profile of one imagined viewer (not 'students' but 'a first-year engineering student in a Pune hostel who wants to cook cheaply and is too shy to ask seniors'). Write the hook and script as if speaking only to that person.
- Single target emotion per piece: Before creating, name the one feeling you want the viewer to have (laughter, empathy, pride, awe, nostalgia, curiosity, relief) and shape the hook, story and pacing around that one feeling instead of mixing several. For video: Pick one target feeling per video and check every choice (hook wording, music, pacing, camera) against it, cutting anything that pulls the other way. Aim for feelings you would be happy to be known for; avoid building videos around anger or disgust.
- Information Gap Theory of Curiosity: Curiosity is triggered by an incomplete 'information set' — when people sense they know part of an answer but not all of it, they feel a genuine, almost physical pull to resolve the gap. Curiosity is weakest when people have no idea about an answer AND when they're fully certain of it; it peaks in between. Four reliable ways to trigger it: posing a question/puzzle, showing a sequence of events with an unknown resolution, violating an expectation (triggering a search for explanation), and revealing that someone else possesses information the audience doesn't. For video: This is the actual mechanism behind curiosity-gap hooks ('you won't believe what happened next'): give the viewer enough of the picture to feel invested, but withhold the resolution. The strongest hook shows a partial result/detail (not zero, not everything) — e.g. show the finished dish's texture but not how it was made, or name an experiment the creator has actually done without revealing the outcome until later in the video.
- Ignition point: The moment a story truly begins isn't the opening scene — it's the specific event that directly challenges the character's core belief about how to control their world (their 'theory of control'), forcing them to react in a revealing, often overreacting way. Everything before this moment is setup; this is where curiosity and tension actually spark. For video: Identify the exact moment in your video where the 'normal' state gets disrupted (the failed attempt, the surprising result, the challenge) and lead with it or move it as early as possible — don't bury the disruption after a long normal-life setup, since that's the moment attention actually switches on.
- Start from milieu/what-if/argument, but land on a specific flawed character: Most story ideas begin from one of three non-character sources — a milieu (an interesting setting), a 'what if' (a premise), or an argument (a point to make) — but these stay generic and clichéd until narrowed down to one specific, flawed individual whose particular damage determines exactly what conflict the plot must create for them. For video: A niche or premise alone ('home workouts', 'budget skincare') isn't a story — narrow it to a specific person's specific struggle (not 'busy people' but 'a Mumbai flat-dweller who's tried and quit three gym memberships') before scripting, matching this dataset's existing 'write for ONE person' principle from Go Viral.
- Status games and moral outrage as engagement drivers: Groups function as 'status games' whose rules are encoded and transmitted through shared stories; content that triggers moral outrage (someone breaking the group's rules) or shows someone earning/losing status pulls on a very old, powerful social-emotional trigger. For video: Content built around a common mistake ('most people get X wrong'), a practice that breaks the group's unwritten rules, or 'how I earned/lost respect by doing Y' (status) taps a deeper and more automatic engagement driver than neutral information alone — use deliberately and honestly, not as manufactured outrage-bait. Never name, shame or target a real individual or brand: aim outrage and status only at ideas, practices or common mistakes. [IDEAS ONLY: never name, shame or target a real individual or brand; aim outrage and status at ideas, practices or common mistakes]
- Cause-and-effect beats: A story moves forward when each moment is triggered by the one before it. Linked beats ('because this happened, so I did that, then this changed') feel like a story; unlinked beats read as a list, and viewers drift away from lists. For video: Script the video as a chain, not a list of tips: every beat should answer 'so what happened next because of that?'. Instead of 'tip 1, tip 2, tip 3', show that trying tip 1 caused a problem, which led to tip 2, which finally produced the result.
- Want versus need: The character (the creator on screen, or the viewer the video speaks to) consciously chases one thing while actually needing something else. The gap between the surface want and the deeper need is what gives a story weight. For video: Name both before scripting: the want the viewer would type into search ('a six-pack, fast') and the need underneath it ('to feel confident, and to stop quitting'). Hook on the want, pay off on the need, so the video lands deeper than the promise in its first line.
- Show, don't tell: Viewers are more convinced by what they see happen and work out for themselves than by what they are told. Narrating a quality ('I was exhausted') is weaker than letting it be observed. For video: Film the action instead of saying it: the alarm snoozed again and again rather than 'I hate mornings', the sweat and shaking arms rather than 'this workout is hard'. Let the voiceover add only what the picture cannot show.
- One meaningful detail: A single specific, human detail suggests a whole life behind it and makes a scene feel real. Generic visuals are forgotten; one particular, slightly unexpected detail is remembered. For video: Pick one telling detail per video and give it a shot of its own: the chipped steel tiffin, the handwritten price list, the sticky note on the fridge. One close-up of that beats a montage of stock-looking b-roll.
- Active hero with a goal: A story needs someone who wants something and does things to get it. Without a goal and choices made in pursuit of it, the content is description, not story. For video: Give the person on screen a clear goal in the first seconds ('I want to cook a full thali on a hostel budget') and show them acting on it, choosing, failing and trying again. A tour of a place or a product with nobody pursuing anything is description; add the goal and it becomes a story.

Content characteristics:
- It's native: Content is tailored to the specific culture, format, and language norms of the platform it's posted on, rather than reposting the same asset everywhere. For video: A hook that works as on-screen text on a Reel may need a completely different pacing/format as a YouTube Short or a long-form intro — don't just resize the same video across platforms.
- It doesn't interrupt: Effective content blends into the viewer's normal browsing/entertainment experience and feels like part of the conversation, not an ad breaking into it. For video: Open videos the way the platform's native creators talk to their audience, not the way a traditional TV ad opens — avoid a 'commercial' tone in the first few seconds.
- It doesn't make demands (often): Most content should be generous — giving value, entertainment, or community connection — without an ask attached; overwhelming the audience with sales pitches erodes trust. For video: Reserve explicit CTAs for a minority of videos; let most videos simply deliver on the hook's promise without pivoting to a sales ask.
- It leverages pop culture / trends: Content that connects to current trends or shared cultural moments feels timely and relatable, increasing engagement. For video: Pairs with the earlier 'newsjacking' concept — tie a niche topic to something currently trending in the broader culture when there's a genuine, non-forced connection.
- It's micro: Small, timely, culturally-relevant snippets keep a brand or creator agile and able to react quickly, rather than relying only on big, infrequent productions. For video: Favor a steady stream of short, timely videos/Shorts alongside occasional bigger productions — agility beats a slow, over-produced release schedule.
- It's consistent and self-aware: Content reinforces a recognizable identity across platforms and stays aware of the brand's/creator's own tone and limits, rather than chasing every trend regardless of fit. For video: Ties to the earlier core-story principle — every video, hook, and CTA should feel recognizably 'you' even as formats vary by platform.
- Replying to real questions is part of the content, not an afterthought: Answering questions and comments in your niche - on your own posts and elsewhere - is itself a form of content that builds presence and surfaces ideas for future videos. For video: Set aside time to genuinely answer comments and questions in your niche's community, and keep a running note of questions that repeat - each one is a candidate for a future video topic.
- One clear next step, not several vague asks: When a piece of content does include a call to action, it works best as a single, specific instruction rather than a list of options that makes the viewer choose. For video: If a video ends with an ask, make it one thing - 'comment your city' or 'save this for later' - instead of stacking follow, comment, share and link-in-bio into the same closing line.
- Improves through testing and response, not a fixed first draft: Content that performs well over time usually got there by being adjusted based on how the audience actually reacted, rather than being planned to perfection before the first post. For video: Treat your first version of a format (a series intro, a caption style) as a draft: watch how comments and watch-time respond, then adjust the next one instead of repeating the same approach unchanged.
- Adaptable across formats: The same underlying story can be told as a short Reel/Short, a longer YouTube video, a carousel or a caption; strong stories change length and pacing per platform without changing the core message. For video: Turn one brand-collab story into a punchy 30-second Reel hook and a longer YouTube walkthrough, keeping the same core message but adjusting pacing and detail for each platform.

Persuasion principles:
- Reciprocity: People feel obligated to return a favor or value they've received. In a hook: Give the viewer something useful (a tip, a laugh, a free resource) before asking for a follow, like, or purchase — the 'jab before the right hook' idea.
- Scarcity: People assign more value to things that are limited in availability or time. In a hook: Time-boxed or limited-availability framing in a hook or CTA ('only works for the next 24 hours', 'limited spots'). [STRUCTURE ONLY: shape the video with this idea, never write urgency or pressure wording for the creator]
- Authority: People are more easily persuaded by sources they perceive as credible or expert. In a hook: Open with the creator's own real credential, experience or a result they have actually shown before giving advice — signals the video is worth trusting. Never invent a credential, a figure or a result.
- Social proof: People look to others' behavior to decide what's correct, especially under uncertainty. In a hook: Showing real comments or results the creator actually has builds trust fast in a hook. A count of other people is used only when the creator gave you that number, never invented.
- Liking: People are more easily persuaded by people they like or feel similar to. In a hook: Relatable framing, shared identity/language (local slang, regional dialect), humor, and authenticity increase a hook's pull.
- Commitment & consistency: Once people commit to something small, they feel pressure to act consistently with that commitment. In a hook: Small early CTAs ('comment DAY 1', 'save this for later') build toward larger asks (subscribe, buy) later in a series. - Unity: Shared group identity ('people like us') deepens persuasion beyond simple liking. In a hook: Framing content around a shared identity ('for Mumbai flat-dwellers', 'for working moms') increases relevance and trust.

Marketing concepts:
- Long Tail of content/PR: Very specific niche topics can reach a small, exact audience directly online; an audience doesn't have to be huge to matter. For video: Instead of broad topics, try a precise one, like 'budget skincare for tier-2 city students' rather than 'skincare tips'. Fewer people search for it, but the ones who do find a video made for their exact problem.
- Thought leadership via direct publishing: Publishing genuine expertise directly to your audience (rather than through media gatekeepers) builds long-term trust and authority. For video: A creator who consistently teaches real, useful expertise builds compounding channel trust that outlasts any single viral hit — supports prioritizing a content pillar/series strategy over one-off videos.
- Reputation monitoring: Actively tracking what's said about you across social platforms and blogs surfaces both opportunities (trending questions) and problems (complaints) early. For video: Creators should monitor their own comments and niche conversations to catch a recurring question or complaint that can become the next video topic — a low-cost, high-relevance content-idea source.
- World Wide Rave (organic multi-community spread): Content spreads virally when several independent communities each find their own reason to share it around the same time, rather than from one single push or channel. For video: Design a video to give multiple distinct audiences (e.g. fitness beginners AND busy parents AND budget-conscious viewers) their own reason to share it, instead of targeting only one angle.
- Content-rich ecosystem over single assets: The depth and usefulness of an entire content ecosystem — not any single polished asset — drives discoverability and credibility. For video: Supports the series/playlist strategy over isolated videos: a channel with a deep, organized back-catalog on a niche out-performs one with scattered unrelated uploads, both for search and for viewer trust.
- Coordinated multi-format storytelling: The same core story or message is adapted across multiple formats (blog, audio, video, forum discussion) to meet different audiences where their consumption habits already are. For video: Repurpose one core hook/story across YouTube long-form, Shorts, and community posts so the same message reinforces itself across touchpoints instead of existing only once.
- Jab, Jab, Jab, Right Hook framework: A boxing metaphor for content cadence: 'jabs' are lightweight, generous content that entertains/engages without asking for anything (builds the relationship), while the 'right hook' is the direct call to action/sales ask. Most content should be jabs; the right hook only lands well after enough jabs have built trust. For video: Plan a content calendar where most videos give pure value with no ask (comedy, tips, entertainment) and only occasionally does a video carry a direct CTA — matches the earlier commitment/consistency and reciprocity persuasion principles already in this dataset.
- Plain words over buzzwords: Writing that leans on vague, inflated buzzwords instead of specific, everyday language actually tells the audience less, not more, and makes the message sound like every other pitch. For video: Drop words like 'premium quality', 'game-changing' or 'must-have' in captions and voiceovers. Say exactly what the product does and for whom, the way you'd explain it to a friend, so it's clear instead of generic-sounding.
- Match the words people actually search: Content gets found when its title and description use the same specific words and phrases the audience types into search, not clever or vague alternatives that only make sense internally. For video: Title and caption a Reel or YouTube video with the plain phrase a viewer would type ('easy hostel breakfast recipe') rather than a witty pun, so the title matches what people looking for that need actually search.
- Plan a series, not one-off posts: Treating content as an ongoing plan built around what the audience needs to know keeps topics connected and easier to follow than deciding each post on the day. For video: Before filming, note the questions your followers keep asking and line up a short run of videos that answer them in order, instead of deciding each video's topic the morning you post it.
- Decide the goal before you script the content: Effective content starts from a clear, specific action you want the viewer to take, and works backward from that action to the story - not the other way around. For video: Before writing a script, decide whether this particular video is meant to get saves, drive DMs, or send people to a link, and shape the hook, body and closing line around that one goal.
- Go deep on a few platforms instead of everywhere: Spreading the same effort across every available platform usually produces weaker results than focusing on the handful of platforms where your specific audience already spends its time. For video: Rather than posting a weaker version of every video to five apps, pick the one or two platforms your audience actually watches on and put your full editing and posting effort there.
- Tailor every application instead of copy-pasting: A generic message sent to many people reads as spam and is easy to ignore; a short message that shows you actually looked at the other person's work is far more likely to be read. For video: When you apply to a campaign on Influora, mention something specific about that brand's product or brief in your pitch instead of reusing the same text for every campaign. The same goes for asking another creator to collab.
- Content mix: five kinds of video: A healthy channel mixes five kinds of content: cornerstone pieces (big, time-heavy, can be cut into many smaller ones), quick tips (simple, frequent, easy to make), teaching videos (build expertise and trust), pure fun (entertainment, used now and then), and hot takes (a contrarian view that starts a conversation, used rarely). For video: Plan the month across all five instead of making every video the same format: an occasional cornerstone video, frequent quick-tip Reels or Shorts, regular teaching videos, some pure fun, and the odd hot take.
- Reorganize, rewrite, retire (getting more from old content): Three ways to get more from content you already made: reorganize (cut one big piece into several smaller ones), rewrite (refresh something that has gone out of date), and retire (take down or clearly flag something that is no longer accurate). For video: Cut one long YouTube video into several Reels or Shorts; add a pinned comment or a follow-up video when an older tutorial is out of date; and unlist or clearly flag a video whose claims are no longer true, so it does not mislead viewers.
- Judge each video by its goal: Different videos have different jobs. A video meant to reach new viewers is judged by views, shares and follows; a video meant to get an action (a sign-up, a click, a purchase) is judged by that action. For video: Decide each video's job before posting, then judge it by the right number: don't call a discovery video a failure because it got few clicks, or a sign-up video a failure because it got fewer views. Each hook's goal (new_viewer_discovery, retention_series, conversion) names that job.

Which structure to use (situation -> the storytelling structure above):
- Quick tip or explanation -> Grab-Story-CTA (micro 3-part structure). Use when: The video must be compact and direct
- Problem-led advice -> Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS). Use when: The viewer has a recognizable pain point
- Transformation -> Before-After-Bridge (BAB). Use when: The result is visually clear
- Tutorial or demonstration -> Three-act structure. Use when: The viewer needs setup, process, and result
- Contrarian opinion -> Problem-Agitate-Solve (PAS). Use when: Challenging a common tactic without overclaiming. Problem: name the common tactic and what it gets wrong. Agitate: show what it costs, honestly, with no invented numbers. Solve: give the replacement and one simple way the viewer can test it themselves
- Trend or breaking topic -> Newsjacking. Use when: The topic is current and the creator has a genuine angle
- Personal experience -> Three-act structure. Use when: The creator's lived experience is relevant. Act one: who they were and what they wanted. Act two: the obstacle they actually hit. Act three: what changed in them, shown in one specific moment rather than a highlight reel
- Longer video -> Freytag's Pyramid (5-part dramatic arc). Use when: The video needs several meaningful beats

Shooting and camera settings (starting points, not laws):
Standing rules:
- On a phone with Pro/manual video, lock white balance to a Kelvin value for talking-head videos (about 5600K for daylight, 3200K for warm bulbs) -- auto white balance can shift skin tone mid-video as hands or a screen change colour. On an auto-only phone, use one light source so auto white balance has nothing to chase.
- In video, stay on the phone's optical lenses (the 0.5x ultrawide, the 1x, and the 2x/3x/3.5x telephoto if the phone has them) and never zoom digitally past them -- move the phone closer instead; digital zoom throws away detail.
- Always wipe the camera lens with a microfiber cloth before recording — micro-scratches and finger oil cause severe highlight halation, worsened by humidity and dust common in Indian outdoor environments.
- Keep ISO as low as you can; if you see grain, add light rather than raising ISO (roughly above 800 on mid-range phones, 1600 on 1-inch sensors).
- Only suggest what the creator's phone can actually do. On an auto-only phone (for example OPPO A78 5G) never suggest 4K60, a shutter speed, ISO or Kelvin values -- use tap-to-focus, exposure lock, the brightness slider and moving the phone or the light instead.
- Give every camera setting with its one-line reason, so the creator learns why (for example: 1/50s because India's 50Hz mains makes lights pulse 100 times a second, and 1/50 catches whole pulses).
- India 50Hz lights: India's mains power is 50Hz, so LED and tube lights pulse 100 times a second. A shutter that is not a multiple of that pulse (for example 30fps at 1/60s) catches the light at different points and shows dark rolling bands. Most phone cameras in auto mode already avoid this; it matters in Pro/manual mode, or when bands appear on screen. Fix: Indoors under mains lights in Pro/manual mode: 25fps at 1/50s, or 50fps at 1/100s (1/200s also works). If bands show on an auto-only camera, switch off the flickering light or use daylight. An app with manual video (for example Open Camera) can lock 25fps on phones that support it.

Light:
- Strong natural daylight (terrace/street), when the sun is low or at an angle, not overhead: Position the subject so the sun acts as a backlight/edge light for separation from the background. Use a reflector or a light-colored wall to bounce fill light onto the face. On a Pro-mode phone, lock the lowest ISO; if no ND filter is available, use a faster shutter to protect highlights.
- Soft natural window light: Position the subject about 30-45 degrees to the window (a starting point), with the window out of frame to the side. Expose for the lit side of the face and let the unlit side fall into gentle shadow for cinematic volume.
- Mixed indoor LED (warm bulb + cool tube light — common in Indian apartments): Pick one light as your face light (a window, a lamp or a ring light -- not the ceiling light alone, which lights from straight above); switch off the lights that clash with it, or keep them only in the background.
- Backlit subject (bright shop or sunset behind them): Reposition the subject if possible. If not, expose entirely for the face using exposure compensation (+EV) or HDR video mode to balance the extreme contrast.
- Harsh midday sun (sun high, your shadow short and right under you): Move into open shade -- the shadow of a building or a tree with open sky in front of you -- so the light on your face is soft and even. If you must stay in the sun, bounce light up onto your face from a white sheet, dupatta or thermocol board held low in front of you, then tap your face to lock exposure and raise brightness a little.
- Golden hour (about the first hour after sunrise and the last hour before sunset): The softest, warmest outdoor light. Face the sun at an angle for a warm glow, or keep it behind you for a bright outline. It changes fast: shoot all the shots of one reel within about 20 minutes, and on a Pro-mode phone lock white balance so the colour does not jump between shots.
- Cloudy or overcast day: Clouds make one big soft light with gentle shadows -- good for faces. It can look flat and a little blue: on a Pro-mode phone lock white balance around 6000-6500K to warm it; add colour or texture in the background for contrast; tap your face to lock exposure so the white sky does not darken you.
- Shade under trees: Sunlight through leaves leaves bright spots on the face -- step to where the shade is even, with no sun spots on you. Light bouncing off leaves can tint skin green: face open sky or a light-coloured wall, and on a Pro-mode phone lock white balance.

Background:
- What makes a good background: Depth (about 1-2m between you and the wall, as a starting point), practical lighting (a small warm lamp as a point of interest), textured surfaces (brick, shelving), and a controlled color palette that complements wardrobe.
- What makes a bad background, and the fix: Domestic clutter, dangling wires, bright windows pulling the eye, high-contrast objects intersecting the subject's head, or flat unlit white walls (kills depth, reads amateurish). Fix: First move yourself or the phone so a clean area is behind you; then turn off the room lights and light only your face with a focused desk lamp, so the background falls into shadow; then, if the phone has one, switch to the telephoto (3x/3.5x) to narrow the view.

Where to put the person and the phone:
- Talking-head (educational/vlog): Medium close-up, chest up, camera at eye level. 1x main at about 0.8-1m, or the 3x telephoto at 2-2.5m if the phone has one and the room allows. Leave about two fingers of headroom between the top of the head and the frame edge.
- Product demonstration (tech/cosmetics): Phone at eye level while you talk; tilt it a little down toward your hands only for the product close-up, so objects held at chest level are clear.

When a shot goes wrong (what you see: why. Fix):
- Black, silhouetted face: A bright window directly behind the subject causes auto-exposure to average the scene, crushing the face into shadow. Fix: Reposition so the window is beside (not behind) the subject, or tap to lock AE specifically on the face and raise EV +0.5 to +1.0.
- Choppy, laggy video in a dim room: Android's Camera2 API automatically drops frame rate (to 10-15fps) in low light to gather more light per frame. Fix: Add a physical light source, or lock FPS manually via a Pro-mode app (Open Camera, Blackmagic Camera).
- Distorted facial features (huge nose, tiny ears): The phone is too close to your face (easy to do on the 0.5x ultrawide). Fix: Move the phone farther than arm's length and reframe; if the phone has a telephoto, it lets you stay back and still frame tight.
- Digital crop jitter / warping edges while walking: Electronic Image Stabilization (EIS) over-compensating in low light. Fix: Disable 'Ultra Steady'/EIS at night and rely on Optical Image Stabilization (OIS) only.

Placing the creator, the phone and the light (fix the scene before the settings; angles are starting points, not laws):
Order of work:
- Read the light, then move the creator, then the phone, then the light, then settings: Look first at where the useful light comes from and whether it is hard or soft -- that is an observation, not an instruction. Then instruct in this order: move the creator so the face gets the light you want, then move the phone, then move or add light, and only then fix the background and the settings.

First move by situation (fix the scene before the settings):
- You are squinting, or direct sun is throwing hard, uncomfortable shadows on your face: first move: Move into continuous shade, or turn your body away from the direct sun, before you change exposure. Why: Squinting and hard sun shadows come from where the light is; changing brightness on the phone does not remove them.
- Your face is dark against a bright window or sky: first move: Change where the phone points or what is behind you so the bright window or sky is not behind your head, or add light on your face (a lamp, or a white card bouncing light back). Do not just darken the whole picture. Why: Darkening the whole frame to save the bright window leaves your face too dark to use; fixing the positions keeps both readable.
- The background behind you is distracting: first move: Move the phone or move yourself before you zoom in or switch lenses. Why: Moving changes what is behind you directly, and the camera's distance from you, not the zoom, is what changes how your face looks.
- The main light is directly overhead (ceiling light or high sun): first move: Move yourself or the light, or bounce light up from low in front of you with a white card or sheet. Tilting or lowering the phone is not a lighting fix. Why: The dark eye-socket and nose shadows come from where the light sits; a different camera angle only shows them differently.
- Close-up shot on a very wide camera held close to your face: first move: Move the phone farther away and use the main 1x camera, or a real optical telephoto if your phone has one; keep your face near the centre and reframe. Why: Being very close makes the nearest features, like the nose, look bigger than they are; a wide camera is only what lets you get that close -- the distance changes the face, not the lens.
- The light is too hard, or too soft and flat: first move: If it is too hard, make the light bigger: diffuse it through a sheer curtain, bounce it off a pale wall, or move into shade. If it is too soft and flat, reduce the fill or use a smaller main light placed more to the side. Why: Softness depends on how big the light looks from your face: a bigger source gives softer shadows, a smaller or more distant one gives harder shadows.
- Lights of different colours are falling on your face: first move: Pick one light as your face light, switch the others off or keep them only in the background, and set white balance for that face light. Why: One white-balance setting cannot make two differently coloured lights look neutral at the same time.
- Wide shot that shows the whole room or location: first move: Check the entire frame -- every light, window and background area -- not just your face. Why: A fix that works for your face may not cover the room behind you once the whole space is in the shot.
- You are moving on camera in low light: first move: Add a steady, continuous light and keep the phone stable before relying on Night mode. Why: Night mode can take several seconds per capture and works best with a steady phone, so movement blurs.

Left and right (always the creator's own, as they face the phone: "your left", "your right"):
- Directional instructions must be relational, not ambiguous left/right: Always use the creator's own left and right as they face the phone: 'the window on your left', 'turn a little to your right'. Never say camera-left or camera-right, because left and right flip once the phone and the creator face each other. Then say where the light sits relative to the face, not just where it is in the room: for example 'keep the window on your left, a little in front of you and slightly above your eyes, and turn your face a little toward it'. Outdoors, also give the sun's direction and height from the creator when known (in front of you, behind your left shoulder, high overhead).

Where the main light sits, measured from the creator's face:
- Near camera axis, 0-15 deg: More even face; shadows fall behind features, less apparent; can look flat if perfectly frontal Phone: Near the source axis; avoid getting so close the nose perspective exaggerates Use when: Clear instructional, beauty, or product content; check glasses reflections and forehead highlights
- Slight side, ~15-30 deg: Mild cheek/nose shaping with both eyes commonly readable Phone: Near face-forward axis, just on shadow-side edge if shaping is desired Use when: Natural talking-head starting point
- Three-quarter side, ~30-60 deg: More visible lit-to-shadow transition and texture Phone: Near face axis or slightly toward key side for broader light, toward shadow side for narrower light Use when: Serious interview or dimensional portrait; use fill if far eye disappears
- Side, near 90 deg: Strong side-to-side split, texture, and shadow Phone: Start near front view; rotate face toward/away from key to alter lit-cheek width Use when: Dramatic or documentary look; unsuitable for even beauty face unless fill added
- Behind, ~120-180 deg (backlight): Rim/hair edge or silhouette; face stays dark unless separate front fill exists Phone: Keep source just outside lens axis to limit flare; use face-side sky, bounce, or small key for facial detail Use when: Sunset, edge separation, silhouette; backlight alone is not a face key
- Directly overhead: Downward eye-socket, nose, and chin shadows Phone: Do not expect camera-height change to fix it; move source/creator or add bounce/fill, or move to open shade Use when: Avoid as sole close face key unless a harsh look is intentional
- Below face: Upward shadows, unnatural 'underlit' effect Phone: Keep phone at intended portrait height; do not lower camera to make low light look natural Use when: Horror/theatrical or motivated screen/desk light; generally avoid for professional face lighting

Window light:
- Creator faces window; phone between creator and window: Window is near-axis key; face is even, often less sculpted Do this: Keep window behind phone or outside frame; if flat, rotate creator 10-25 deg toward window; check for reflections in glass
- Creator is 30-45 deg to window: Best general starting point for dimensional talking-head light Do this: Stand near window, face turns partway toward it, camera near front axis; keep 1-2m from background as a practical starting point; add negative fill/dark wall if face too flat
- Creator is 90 deg to window: Strong side light Do this: Camera near front; creator turns toward/away from window to adjust lit cheek and shadow; add white fill if far eye disappears
- Window behind creator toward phone: High-contrast backlight; face may silhouette, window panes may clip Do this: Change camera axis so window moves to side, close/cover curtain, or add controlled face key; do not just underexpose the whole frame; wide shots are hardest here
- Window beside creator: Useful side key if camera sees both face planes Do this: Control shadow with wall bounce or negative fill; background should not have an equally bright pane merging with the head
- Window above creator: Treated as top source; expect eye-socket/nose shadow Do this: Move away from the vertical window beam, use front-side bounce, or turn to a lower side window; camera tilt does not fix top-light geometry
- Multiple windows: One is brightest and should be the key; others may fill or compete Do this: Curtain/cover competing windows if shadows conflict; make face/background exposure work for the intended frame

Lights in an Indian home:
- Window/balcony daylight: Often the most useful large source; make it side/front-side key; switch off clashing room lights with visibly different color.
- Ceiling LED/tube/fan light: As the only face source it's usually top-down, which puts shadows in the eye sockets, under the nose and under the chin; move to a window or wall bounce, add soft camera-side fill, or switch it off if another key exists. Do not just lower camera height.
- Warm bulb/table/floor lamp: Use as an in-frame practical or bounce off a pale wall/ceiling; a bare small bulb creates sharp shadows; keep safe clearance, never cover a hot fixture with fabric.
- Ring light: Gives near-axis even face exposure but can flatten depth and reflect as a ring in glasses; raise it slightly or move off axis for shape and check the reflection.
- TV/display as light source: Changing content changes face brightness/color moment to moment; treat as ambient effect only, not a reliable sole key.
- Kitchen/corridor mixed lights: Commonly mix color temperatures; pick one color family for the face and turn off conflicting sources, or keep them in background as intentional accents.

Mixed light colours:
- Window daylight + warm room lamp: Use the window as your face light. Switch the lamp off, or move it behind you as a warm lamp in the background. If your phone allows it, lock white balance on your face and the background lamp can stay warm if you like the look; otherwise switch the lamp off.
- Cool ceiling LED + warm bulb: Use the warm bulb (or a window) for your face and switch the ceiling LED off; if the ceiling light is the only light you have, switch off the warm bulb and bounce light up onto your face with a white card or sheet held low in front of you.
- Two daylight windows: Use the brighter window as your face light, and curtain or cover the other one if it throws shadows in a different direction.
- Phone auto white balance shifts during the take: Lock or set white balance if your phone allows it. If it does not, cut down the competing light colours and do not walk through areas lit by different colours during the shot.
- Sunset into blue hour: Lock white balance if your phone allows it, so your shots match, or change it deliberately once between scenes. Add a steady face light as the daylight drops.

Phone height:
- Eye-level: Neutral point of view, reliable eye contact Use when: Best default for interviews, education, direct address
- Slightly above eyes: Sees more forehead/top planes, less underside Use when: Mild beauty/style choice; too high can feel diminutive and distort proportions in medium/wide
- Slightly below eyes: Sees more chin/nostril underside, more imposing viewpoint Use when: Use deliberately; do not use to 'fix' overhead light
- High angle: Shows floor/table and top of head; can reduce perceived scale Use when: Overhead hands-only shots, or an intentional emotional perspective
- Low angle: Shows ceiling/underside, increases perceived scale Use when: Heroic/dramatic choice; can expose ceiling fixtures and clutter

Fixing the background (first fix, then the next one):
- Messy room/clutter: first: Move creator/camera so only a clean zone is visible; remove or conceal distracting objects. Then: Use closer framing or a longer optical view if distance/light permit.
- Blank wall: first: Move creator away from wall; add one meaningful practical, shelf, or side light. Then: Change camera angle to create depth; do not add random decor.
- Bright window in background: first: Rotate camera/window off axis, close/diffuse curtain, or move creator to shade. Then: Add face key or expose for face if window clipping is visually acceptable.
- Mirror/glass reflection: first: Move phone/light off the reflection axis; adjust angle a few degrees. Then: Change background or cover the reflective surface.
- Bright object merging with head: first: Shift camera or creator sideways until the object no longer merges with the head. Then: Lower, dim, or move the object.
- Subject blends into background: first: Change background tone/distance or add backlight. Then: Use side key/negative fill to create tonal edge separation.

Portrait lighting patterns:
- Rembrandt-style: Key roughly 30-45 deg horizontally and above face; turn face slightly away until far-cheek triangle appears. Caution: The triangle is a visual result, not guaranteed by the nominal angle -- check it through the camera.
- Loop: Key above and off-axis enough to create a small nose shadow angled downward and away from the nose. Caution: Ensure the shadow does not merge with the cheek shadow.
- Butterfly: Key close to camera axis and high enough to create a small downward nose shadow. Caution: Common beauty pattern; check eye-socket depth and glasses reflections.
- Split: Key near 90 deg to face; face square or adjusted until light/shadow division is intentional. Caution: Strong contrast; add fill if the shadow side loses necessary detail.
- Backlight/rim: Source behind at a side angle; face key/fill on camera-side if detail is needed. Caution: Keep source off lens axis; control flare and shoulder spill.

Sunset to night, step by step (timing depends on your location and date, not the clock; recheck your face and background every few minutes as the sun drops):
- 1. Low sun: Decide between three looks: sun on your face, sun behind one shoulder as a rim of light, or a silhouette. If the sun is on your face, keep it out of your eyes so you do not squint. If it is behind you as a rim, face toward the open sky or a white card on the phone's side of you.
- 2. Sun near the horizon: Shift the phone a little so the sun itself is not in line with the lens, unless you want flare or a silhouette. If the rim of light disappears, reframe against a darker background.
- 3. Falling daylight: Bring a soft light closer to your face or move to a brighter spot. Do not assume the phone will keep a clean picture on its own -- expect grain and motion blur.
- 4. Blue hour: Keep the phone steady, use the main camera, light your face from near the phone, and let distant lamps and signs become points of light in the background.
- 5. Dark: If you move on camera, add a steady continuous light. Night mode works best with the phone held still for several seconds, so it is not a substitute for real light when there is movement.

Why it works:
- Apparent source size determines softness, not source type: A source's softness depends on its apparent size relative to the subject, not what it 'is' -- the sun is physically enormous but so distant it acts as a small hard source; a large nearby window or bounce surface appears larger and produces softer shadows. A curtain can turn a small bright opening into a broad diffuse source.
- Inverse-square falloff (point sources) vs. large soft sources: For a point-like source, doubling the distance yields about one-quarter the illuminance (a two-stop loss). Large soft sources behave differently at close range -- ASC measurements show a gentler initial falloff. Do not apply the point-source rule blindly to a large softbox or close window.
- Camera distance, not focal length, changes facial perspective: Camera-to-subject distance changes perspective (how features appear relative to each other); focal length mainly changes field of view. A 'compressed' telephoto look results from moving farther away and reframing with a longer lens -- a longer lens does not itself compress perspective if camera position is unchanged.
- Clock time is not a sun direction: Solar noon is not necessarily clock noon; sun azimuth/elevation must be derived from location, date, and time (e.g. via NOAA's solar calculator), not assumed from the clock alone. Do not plan a shot around a clock time alone; check where the sun is for your place and date.

How to deliver the lines (how the creator SAYS a line: stress, pauses, pace, energy, gestures. Every tip is optional and relative to the creator's own voice):
Delivery guardrails (always):
- Never give a fixed words-per-minute, pitch, loudness, pause length, gesture count or eye-contact target.
- Judge pace, energy, pitch and pauses against the creator's own usual voice, when you have it.
- Never claim a delivery tip causes more retention, completion, shares, follows, reach or virality.
- Never treat an accent, a dialect, mixing languages, where someone looks or how their face moves as a measure of skill, honesty, health or professionalism.
- Every delivery tip is optional and the creator can change it.

Delivery rules:
- Dense line: If a line packs several ideas or unfamiliar terms, split it into two short lines, or pause at the natural break between its parts -- never inside a tight phrase. Limits: Does not prove that shorter sentences increase retention. (Source confidence: high for the studied comprehension contexts.)
- Pace against their own normal: If the creator talks clearly faster than their own usual pace on a dense line, suggest slowing a little on that line and saying each word clearly, or splitting it. Never give a words-per-minute target. Limits: No universal ideal WPM is established. (Source confidence: high for studied intelligibility/processing outcomes; low for platform outcomes.)
- One stressed phrase: In each line, pick the one word or short phrase that carries the new, contrasting or payoff information and suggest stressing it a little -- held slightly longer, with a small rise or fall in the voice. No loudness or pitch numbers. Limits: No fixed dB or pitch target; do not transfer one language's contour to all Indian languages. (Source confidence: high for general prominence; medium beyond studied Hindi tasks.)
- Pause where the meaning breaks: Pause at the end of a sentence, a big clause or a change of topic; a breath there is fine. A pause in the wrong place can change the meaning, and silence is not good by itself. Limits: A pause at an incorrect boundary can disrupt meaning; silence is not automatically beneficial. (Source confidence: high for boundary relevance; no universal duration.)
- No pause lengths in seconds: Never give a pause length in seconds; say what the pause is for -- end of a sentence, suspense before a reveal, after a punchline, before the call to action. Limits: A later product may estimate timing from consented creator rehearsal data and validate it.
- Hinglish stays Hinglish: When a line mixes Hindi and English, keep the mix exactly as the creator speaks it; never 'correct' it into one language. If the Roman spelling of a word, a borrowed word or a name is unclear, ask them. Limits: Do not generalize a Hindi-English corpus to every Indian language pair or creator genre. (Source confidence: high for Hindi-English corpus and processing evidence.)
- Accent is not an error: Never call an accent or dialect an error. Offer a different pronunciation only if the creator asks, and only for a word their audience may find hard to understand. Limits: Functional understanding is listener- and context-dependent; do not infer disorder from an automated signal.
- Energy relative to their normal: If the creator wants more energy or a calmer tone, describe it relative to how they usually sound (a little more engaged, a little calmer); never a loudness or pitch number. Limits: Room gain, microphone distance, compression, emotion, and health affect measured level. (Source confidence: high for relative calibration principle.)
- Gesture on the same phrase: Where a line names something visible, or on its stressed phrase, you may suggest one simple gesture or expression on that same phrase (three fingers on 'teen', pointing at the product). It is optional: skip it if the framing or the creator's preference does not suit it, and never ask for constant eye contact or constant gestures. Limits: Not an established retention rule; do not prescribe constant gaze or gesture. (Source confidence: moderate in studied tasks.)
- Platform guidance is labelled: When you pass on a platform's own creative advice, say it is that platform's guidance and roughly when it was published, keep it apart from research, and do not treat advice for paid ads as advice for normal posts. Limits: Paid-ad creative advice should not automatically be generalized to organic videos. (Source confidence: high that it is platform guidance; performance effect varies or is unestablished.)
- A drop is a question, not a verdict: When a drop or jump in viewers lines up with part of a script, call it a possible reason and suggest one small change to test; never say that line caused it. Limits: Topic, traffic source, visual edit, sound, captions, audience, and distribution can confound the pattern. (Source confidence: high for interpretation limit.)
- Voice strain: If the creator mentions hoarseness, pain, strain, tiring fast, frequent throat clearing or losing vocal range, never suggest pushing volume, whispering or forcing pitch or speed; suggest a qualified doctor if it continues, and never diagnose. Limits: The AI cannot diagnose a voice disorder from script or audio alone. (Source confidence: high for safety guidance.)
